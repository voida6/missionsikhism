## Forty in a mud house

The party that came away from [the Sirsa](/events/evacuation-of-anandpur) reached [Chamkaur](/places/chamkaur) with around forty men and took up position in a *garhi* — a walled mud house on rising ground, not a fort in any serious sense. The pursuing force arrived behind them in numbers the sources put in the thousands.{{1,5}}

They fought it in sorties: small parties going out from the house a few at a time. The Guru's elder sons, Ajit Singh at about seventeen and Jujhar Singh at about fourteen, both went out and were both killed. Their father, on the accounts, watched from the wall and did not stop them.{{1,2}}

## The order

By nightfall the position could not hold. What happened next is the part of the story the tradition holds most carefully, and it is a constitutional event as much as a military one.

The surviving Sikhs resolved that the Guru must leave, and they did not ask him. They instructed him — as the [Khalsa](/glossary/khalsa), by *gurmata*, exercising the authority he had himself vested in the five when [he founded it in 1699](/events/creation-of-the-khalsa). And he obeyed.{{1,5}}

He left the *garhi* that night with two companions while a Sikh named Sangat Singh put on the Guru's own robes and plume and remained behind, so that the besiegers would believe he was still inside.

## Why this is the entry that matters

Chamkaur is a defeat. Two of his sons were killed at it and he escaped it on foot into the Machhiwara jungle, alone by the end.

It is nevertheless the moment where the thing built at Anandpur in 1699 is shown actually working. The Guru had said the Khalsa was the Guru's own form and that he was bound by it; five years later, cornered and having just lost two children, he was ordered out by five Sikhs in a mud house and he went. The tradition does not read this as him being saved. It reads it as him keeping his word.

## The letter

From the Malwa the following year he wrote to Aurangzeb in Persian verse. The *[Zafarnama](/library)* gives his own account of this battle across verses 19 to 41 — including the disparity of numbers, which he states plainly — and it is one of the very few places in Sikh history where the central figure narrates his own defeat.{{3}}

The title is not ironic. He called it the Epistle of Victory.
