## Ten years of collapse

[Ranjit Singh](/events/ranjit-singh-captures-lahore) died in 1839 leaving a large, well-armed state and no working mechanism for succession. What followed was a decade of court killings: four rulers in six years, ministers murdered in the palace, and a Khalsa army that had become a political actor in its own right, negotiating with a darbar it no longer trusted.{{2}}

The First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–46) ended in defeat and the Treaty of Lahore, which took territory, cut the army and installed a British Resident. The Second (1848–49) ended it. On 29 March 1849 the Punjab was annexed to the East India Company's territory.{{1,3}}

## What was taken

The Maharaja Dalip Singh, Ranjit Singh's youngest son, was ten years old. He was deposed, separated from his mother Jind Kaur, converted to Christianity, and sent to England, where he lived as a curiosity of the Victorian court. As part of the terms of surrender he handed over the Koh-i-Noor diamond to Queen Victoria.{{2,4}}

The Punjab was the last major Indian territory to fall to the Company. It had also been the hardest — a point the British registered in their own way.

## The colonial relationship, and what it did

Annexation is not the end of the Sikh story in the way a defeat usually is, because of what the British did next.

Within a decade the Company was recruiting Sikhs heavily into its army, especially after 1857, when Punjab troops stayed loyal during the Rebellion. The colonial doctrine of *martial races* classified Sikhs as natural soldiers, and the army rewarded — and in practice required — the outward Khalsa form: uncut hair, turban, the Five Ks. A Sikh recruit was expected to be, and to look, a Khalsa Sikh.{{1}}

The effect was double-edged and it is worth stating both halves. It carried Sikhs across the empire — [to Malaya and Singapore as police](/places/singapore), [to East Africa on the railways](/events/makindu-gurdwara-built), to Hong Kong, Shanghai and beyond — seeding much of the diaspora that exists today. It also meant that a colonial state's preference became a powerful force in defining who counted as properly Sikh, at exactly the moment [the Singh Sabha](/events/founding-of-singh-sabha) was arguing that question out among Sikhs themselves.{{1}}

**What is disputed.** How much of modern Sikh identity is a product of that colonial encounter is one of the sharpest arguments in the field. That the British shaped the terms is not seriously contested; whether they largely *made* the boundary between Sikh and Hindu, or merely hardened one already forming, is. The [Singh Sabha entry](/events/founding-of-singh-sabha) takes that up directly.
