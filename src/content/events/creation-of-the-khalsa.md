## The call for a head

The assembly at [Anandpur](/events/founding-of-anandpur) that Vaisakhi was vast — the spring gathering when the community came to its Guru. What happened there is the most dramatised scene in Sikh memory: the Guru emerged sword in hand and asked for a Sikh willing to give his head. One by one, five volunteers followed him into a tent — Daya Ram, Dharam Das, Himmat Rai, Mohkam Chand, Sahib Chand — and each time the Guru returned with a bloodied blade, until the five stood revealed alive and robed as the Panj Pyare, the Five Beloved Ones.

**What the sources actually say.** The earliest surviving account is Sainapati's *Sri Gur Sobha*, written by a poet of the Guru's own court around 1711. It records the Guru's dramatic challenge to the assembly — but it does not tell the story of the heads and the tent in the form above.{{1}} That detailed narrative first appears in Kuir Singh's *Gurbilas Patshahi 10* of 1751, half a century later, and is elaborated further by Sukkha Singh, Santokh Singh and the writers who followed.{{2}} Even the order of the five differs: older sources place Mohkam Singh second, later ones move him to fourth.{{7}}

This does not make the scene false. It means the tradition preserved the meaning of the day with confidence and filled in its staging over time — which is what oral memory does. The institution founded that Vaisakhi is not in doubt by any historian.

Even the day is unsettled: the Julian-to-Gregorian conversion puts the 1699 Vaisakhi at 30 March, while the festival is now kept on 13 or 14 April.{{6}}

## Amrit and the double initiation

The five were initiated with *khande di pahul*, nectar stirred with a double-edged sword — and then, in the act the tradition treasures most, the Guru knelt and took initiation from his own initiates.{{1,4}} A Guru receiving from disciples inverted the entire logic of religious authority in the region.

Male initiates took the name Singh, lion; female initiates Kaur, princess. Caste names were set aside, and with them the information a name carried about a person's birth. The initiated were bound to a discipline — the ancestor of the modern [Rehat Maryada](/faith/rehat-maryada) and of the [amrit ceremony](/faith/ceremonies-and-rites) as practised now.{{5,7}}

**What is disputed.** How much of the later code was specified in 1699 itself. The Five Ks are not set out as a fixed list in the earliest sources; the *rahit* appears in written form only in the eighteenth-century rahitnamas, which disagree with one another on details, and the settled modern text was not agreed until the Rehat Maryada of 1945.{{5}} The direction of travel is clear from the start; the precise content was worked out by the community over two centuries.

## Sovereignty in a community's hands

[Khalsa](/glossary/khalsa) means "the pure" — and, in the Persian revenue vocabulary the Mughal administration used every day, *crown lands*: territory held directly by the sovereign with no intermediary lord in between.{{3}} Both senses were available to everyone present. The Guru had placed the community under no intermediary authority at all.

That had a concrete institutional edge. The *masands*, the deputies who had collected offerings and mediated between the Guru and distant congregations, had become a problem of corruption and rival patronage; the Khalsa dispensation cut them out, binding initiates directly to the Guru.{{3,4}}

Nine years later the design was completed. The Guru [vested eternal Guruship in the scripture](/events/guru-granth-eternal-guruship) and left the Khalsa itself as the Guru's body in the world — a book and a community, with no single person for any state to arrest.
