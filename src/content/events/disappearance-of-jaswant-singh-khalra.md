## The decade this comes out of

The years between [1984](/events/anti-sikh-violence-1984) and the mid-1990s were the worst in Punjab's modern history, and the violence ran in more than one direction. A militant separatist movement killed civilians — Hindu and Sikh — in large numbers. The state's counter-insurgency killed civilians in large numbers too, and did so with legal powers that made disappearance straightforward and accountability close to impossible.

Anyone writing about this decade honestly has to hold both of those at once. This entry is about the second, because of what one man did about it.

## The firewood

Jaswant Singh Khalra was a bank official in [Amritsar](/places/amritsar), not an activist by trade. He began looking into the disappearances of young men in his district after people close to him vanished, and he went at it as someone used to records.

He worked from municipal cremation ledgers, and from the purchase records for the firewood the crematoria used. The wood was the tell: consumption that could not be explained by the bodies officially accounted for. Following it, he established that large numbers of bodies had been cremated by police as "unidentified" or "unclaimed", without inquest, notification of families, or record of who they had been.

He made the finding public in January 1995: on his figures, some 2,000 such cremations in Amritsar district alone, with the pattern extending across Punjab.{{2}}

## 6 September 1995

He was washing his car outside his house in Amritsar when Punjab Police personnel took him. The courts later established that he was held at Jhabal police station, tortured, shot, and his body disposed of in the Sutlej near the Harike bridge. It was never recovered.{{2}}

## What followed

In November 2005, ten years on, six Punjab Police officials were convicted of his abduction and murder. Two were sentenced to life; on appeal in 2007 the sentences of four were increased to life.{{2}}

The Central Bureau of Investigation's own inquiry confirmed over 2,000 unlawful cremations in the Tarn Taran area alone — which is to say the investigation he was killed for conducting was substantially vindicated by the state's own investigators.{{2}}

At least four lawyers and two journalists were killed while continuing the work.{{2}}

## Why this entry and not another

The disappearances of that decade are counted in the thousands and almost none of them produced a conviction. Human Rights Watch's assessment of the 1984 cases — 30 convictions, no police officer among them — describes the same machinery working the same way a decade later.{{3}}

The Khalra case is the exception, and it is in this chronicle for that reason rather than in spite of it. It is the one instance where a Sikh killed by the Punjab Police in that period was named, the killing was proved in court, and serving officers went to prison for it. He is widely remembered as *Shaheed* — martyr — and the reason the word fits is not that he died, but what he died holding: a set of numbers, taken from public records, that the state could not make untrue.
