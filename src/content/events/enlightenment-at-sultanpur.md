## The steward of Sultanpur

Before he was a Guru, Nanak was a civil servant. In his late twenties he moved to Sultanpur Lodhi — a district headquarters for Daulat Khan Lodi, who would go on to become one of the most powerful political figures in the Punjab — and worked there for roughly a decade as storekeeper of the granary, a post arranged through his brother-in-law Jai Ram.{{2,4}} He was married, had two sons, and drew a salary from the Sultanate's revenue system.

This matters for reading everything he later said. The man who dismissed ritual, caste and hollow religious authority had spent ten years inside a state administration, weighing grain and keeping accounts. His hymns are full of the vocabulary of that world — shops, bazaars, coins, weighing, debt.{{2}} The janam-sakhis describe the same decade in a quieter register: work by day, and devotional singing before dawn with Mardana, a Muslim *mirasi* musician named in the earliest sources as one of the small circle around him.{{6}}

## Three days at the river

Tradition holds that one morning, around 1499 and aged about thirty, Nanak went to bathe in the Bein — a slow tributary running past the town — and did not come out. His clothes were on the bank; searchers found nothing; he was given up for drowned. On the third day he reappeared.{{5,6}} He was silent for a day, and then said the words the tradition treats as the charter of the faith: *na koi Hindu, na koi Musalman* — there is no Hindu, there is no Muslim.{{4}} The janam-sakhis describe the intervening time as an audience in the divine court, where he was given a cup of nectar and charged with the ministry of the Name.{{6}}

**What is disputed.** The episode is not attested by any contemporary record. It appears only in the janam-sakhis, whose surviving manuscripts postdate the Guru's death, and the details vary between recensions — three days in the Puratan tradition, longer in some later versions.{{1,2}} McLeod placed the disappearance among the episodes he could not accept as literal history, reading it as a hagiographic convention common to medieval saints' lives.{{1}} Nor does the Guru Granth Sahib, which preserves Nanak's own compositions, narrate the event or claim any miracle for him.{{1}}

What is not in dispute is that something changed. Nanak resigned a secure government post, gave away his possessions, and left. Scholars who differ sharply about the janam-sakhis agree that the community's memory of a decisive commissioning is anchored to Sultanpur, and that from this point he taught as one sent.{{1,3}}

The saying itself has been read two ways, and both readings survive in the tradition. Taken as sociology it rejects the religious labels of his day. Taken as theology it claims that neither community's practice, as he found it, was actually reaching God. Nanak's own compositions support the second reading more strongly than the first — his quarrel is with empty observance, not with either faith's existence.{{3,7}}

## The journeys

Nanak spent much of the next two decades travelling. The Puratan tradition records four long journeys — the *udasis* — south toward Sindh, north to the Himalayan foothills, west to Mecca, and north-west toward what is now Peshawar.{{2}} Later janam-sakhis extend the range considerably, adding Assam, Sri Lanka and Baghdad; the further the destination, the later the text that first mentions it, which is itself a caution.{{2}}

He travelled with Mardana and disputed with Shaivite and Vaishnava ascetics, Sufis, Nath yogis and Brahmins. According to the Puratan he did more than argue: he initiated followers with *charanamrit*, organised congregations in the places he passed through, and appointed *manjis* — local seats of authority responsible for a congregation's daily routine.{{2}} If that account is right, the institution-building began on the road rather than at the end of it, which cuts against the older scholarly picture of a purely itinerant preacher who only later settled down.{{1,2}}

The message he carried is set out across [the Faith section](/faith/foundations-philosophy): [one God](/faith/one-god), no caste before the divine, honest work, and remembrance of the Name. The travelling ended where settled community began — [at Kartarpur](/events/founding-of-kartarpur).
