## Hawkers, then bananas

Punjabi men reached the New South Wales north coast in the early twentieth century, most of them working as itinerant hawkers because that was what the law and the labour market left open. Under the White Australia policy, in force from 1901 into the 1970s, they could not hold freehold title to land — so they worked ground they were not permitted to own.

As the restrictions eased they moved into banana growing around [Woolgoolga](/places/woolgoolga). By the 1990s Punjabi families held the majority of the plantations on that coast.{{1}}

## A gurdwara that looks like the town

The First Sikh Temple on Hastings Street opened on 6 June 1968, the ceremony performed by a serving Australian senator.{{1}} What is notable is the architecture: open plan, brick and tile, with a langar hall in the same materials — deliberately built in the local vernacular rather than in the domed idiom that gurdwaras elsewhere in the diaspora adopted.{{1,2}}

That was a choice about belonging, made by a community that had spent seventy years being told it did not. A second and much larger gurdwara, in a more recognisably Punjabi style, was added later — the two buildings between them marking a shift in how confidently the community expected to be read.

## A Sikh country town

Woolgoolga is unusual anywhere in the diaspora. Sikhs form a substantial majority of a town of roughly six thousand people — not a neighbourhood within a city but the town itself, with the gurdwara functioning as a community centre for Sikh and non-Sikh residents alike.{{1}}

Australia's Sikh population has since grown very fast, largely through skilled and student migration to Melbourne, Sydney and Brisbane. The 2021 census counted 210,400 Sikhs — 0.8 per cent of the country, almost triple the 2011 figure, with a median age of 30.{{3}} Almost all recent Sikh arrivals were born in India.{{3}}
