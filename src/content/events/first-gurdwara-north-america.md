## Built by hand, on a hillside

Sikh settlers reached British Columbia's Fraser Valley from about 1905, working the sawmills and farms. In 1908 the community at [Abbotsford](/places/abbotsford) secured a hillside plot, and carried the timber up from the mills themselves. The Gur Sikh Temple opened on 26 February 1911.{{1,2}}

Two earlier Canadian gurdwaras — Golden in 1905, Vancouver's Kitsilano in 1908 — no longer stand, which leaves Abbotsford as the oldest surviving gurdwara anywhere in North America.{{2}} In 2002 it became a National Historic Site of Canada: the only gurdwara outside India and Pakistan to hold such a designation.{{1,3}}

## The law they built it under

The timing is the part usually left out. The building went up in the middle of Canada's effort to shut Indian migration down altogether — the *Continuous Journey* regulation of 1908, which required migrants to travel from their country of origin without stopping at a time when no shipping line ran direct from India. It was designed to be impossible to satisfy, and it was the instrument used to turn away the *Komagata Maru* and its 376 passengers at Vancouver in 1914.

So the men carrying lumber up that hill were building a permanent institution in a country actively legislating against their presence. The building is the argument.

## What came of it

Canada now has the largest Sikh population outside India — 771,790 at the 2021 census, around 2.1 per cent of the country, and more than England and Australia combined. Sikhs are recorded in every province and territory; British Columbia, where the Abbotsford community started, holds 290,870 of them.{{4}}

The Gur Sikh Temple is still in use, with a Sikh Heritage Museum on the ground floor.
