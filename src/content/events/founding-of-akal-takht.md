## A throne facing the temple

Guru Hargobind was eleven when his father was [executed at Lahore](/events/martyrdom-of-guru-arjan). What he built in response was a piece of architecture that argued a position.

Across the precinct from the Harmandir Sahib — which sits deliberately low, approached by steps down — he raised a platform and, on it, a seat: the Akal Takht, the throne of the Timeless One. Tradition holds that only Baba Buddha and Bhai Gurdas were permitted to work on its construction.{{5}} The Harmandir was where the community met God; the Takht was where it conducted its worldly business. Petitions were heard there, disputes settled, envoys received, and — the detail that mattered to the Mughal administration — weapons and horses accepted as offerings in place of cash.{{1,2}}

The two buildings face each other. That was the argument.

## Miri and piri

At his succession the young Guru is said to have set aside the *seli*, the woollen cord his predecessors wore, and girded two swords instead: one for spiritual authority (*piri*), one for temporal (*miri*).{{2,5}} This is the doctrine of [miri-piri](/glossary/miri-piri), and the Takht is its statement in brick — the claim that a religious community may legitimately hold and exercise worldly power, rather than submitting to whoever else holds it.

It is worth being clear about what changed and what did not. The Panth had already been operating as a power centre at [Ramdaspur](/events/founding-of-amritsar) under Guru Arjan, with its own town, revenue and court; that is a large part of why Jahangir moved against him.{{4}} What 1606 added was not power but *declared* power — and an armed retinue to go with it. The *Dabistan-i-Mazahib*, whose author knew Guru Hargobind personally, describes the sixth Guru's followers as soldiers within his own lifetime, which puts the militarisation beyond argument from a source with no Sikh axe to grind.{{3}}

**What is disputed.** The date. Most accounts give 1606, some run as late as 1609, and no contemporary document fixes it.{{5}} The founding is secure; the year is conventional.

## The community's parliament

Through the eighteenth century the Takht was where the scattered warbands of the [misl period](/history/misl-period) assembled as the Sarbat Khalsa to pass binding collective resolutions — *gurmatas* — the closest thing a persecuted and decentralised community had to a parliament.{{1,5}} Its custodians issued *hukamnamas*, directives carrying authority to congregations far beyond Punjab.

It remains the senior seat among the five Takhts of the faith. That is why its shelling in [June 1984](/events/operation-blue-star) was experienced not as damage to a building but as an assault on Sikh sovereignty itself — and why the community insisted on demolishing the government's reconstruction and rebuilding it by its own hands.
