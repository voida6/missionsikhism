## From wanderer to householder

When his journeys ended, Guru Nanak did something deliberate: he put on the clothes of a householder, took up farming on the right bank of the Ravi, and gathered a community around him. Kartarpur — "the town of the Creator" — became the working demonstration of the teaching. The choice was doctrinal as much as practical. Against the renunciant traditions all around him, Nanak held that liberation is pursued inside ordinary life, and he was blunt about the alternative: the idler pierces his ears to pass for a yogi, and the man who turns beggar throws away his standing — *don't ever touch his feet*.{{8}}

He did not pick the spot at random. His father-in-law, Mula Chona, worked for Ajita Randhawa, a Jat chief in the area, so the Guru knew the ground and had a way in.{{2}} The land where the Ravi comes down into the plains is fertile, well-watered and close enough to the Shivalik foothills to sit on established pilgrimage routes — which meant a steady traffic of exactly the people most open to a new way of living.{{2}} The Puratan tradition says he first tried something similar at his home village of Talwandi and moved on, plausibly because a village built around a mosque and a temple was a difficult place to grow a third thing.{{6}}

**What is disputed.** Popular accounts often date the founding to 1504. The scholarly consensus of the last century placed it at the *end* of the Guru's travels, in the early 1520s, which is the dating this site follows. But the Puratan Janam-sakhi — the earliest source — says Kartarpur was established after the *first* journey, and that the Guru went on travelling afterwards.{{2}} If the Puratan is right, the community was not a retirement project but the base of operations for most of his ministry.

## The pattern of a Sikh day

The Kartarpur community kept a rhythm the faith still recognises: rising before dawn for meditation on the Name (*nam japna*), the day given to honest labour (*kirat karni*), and food shared without distinction of caste in the common kitchen (*vand chhakna*, expressed in the [langar](/glossary/langar)). Those three obligations are the durable core of what Nanak left behind, and every later development of Sikh practice is built on them.{{3,4}} The Guru put the principle in a single line: *one who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has — he knows the Path.*{{8}}

The Puratan describes the shape of it concretely. Sikhs gathered at the Guru's *dargah* — his house — recited his compositions as their daily prayer, heard him expound them, and ate together.{{6}} That implies a substantial establishment: room for congregational worship, a kitchen able to feed a crowd, and lodging for visitors, because Sikhs in distant places were expected to come.{{2}} The Guru's house was the community's sacred place; the daily prayer and the shared meal were what marked its members off from the Shaivites, Vaishnavas, Sufis and Sunnis among whom they lived.{{2,6}}

**Where historians disagree.** McLeod devoted a page and a half to these two decades, and read Nanak's system as "a reworking of the Sant synthesis" — the categories, terminology and doctrine of the north Indian Sants — calling him a founder only "in a highly qualified sense".{{1}} Mann's objection is that this leaves the central fact unexplained: of all the Sant teachers of the period, Nanak was the only one who built an institution, and he built a detailed one.{{2}} The disagreement is not about what happened at Kartarpur so much as about how much of it counts as new.

## A kingdom, a coin, a book

The people at Kartarpur did not describe it as a retreat. The earliest account, composed by the court musicians Rai Balwand and Satta within a generation of the Guru's death and later included in scripture, puts it in the language of statecraft: *Nanak established the kingdom; he built the true fortress on the strongest foundations.*{{5}} Bhai Gurdas used the same register a century later — Nanak struck his coin in the world, and the Nirmal Panth set out.{{7}} The Goindval Pothis of the 1570s call him the "Bedi King", supporting both *din* and *duniya*, religion and the world.{{2}}

Three instruments held it together: the Guru's own authority at the centre, the *manjis* representing that authority in distant congregations, and a *pothi* — a bound book of his compositions, written in the Gurmukhi script he had shaped for the purpose.{{2}} That last point is contested. McLeod's generation held that scripture as a Sikh institution began with [Guru Arjan](/events/installation-of-adi-granth) in 1604; Mann argues that Nanak wrote his compositions into a book and handed it on ceremonially, which would move the origin of Sikh scripture to the founder himself.{{1,2}}

## Succession, not inheritance

Guru Nanak died at Kartarpur in 1539. Before his death he passed over his sons and installed his follower Lehna as successor, renaming him Angad — "of my own limb". Balwand and Satta record the moment with the detail that gives it its force: *the Guru bowed to his disciple, while Nanak was still alive* — the ceremonial mark applied by a living king.{{5}}

The act established that Guruship followed merit and spirit rather than blood, and it carried the young community through [nine further successions](/history/guru-period). Kartarpur itself was later lost to the shifting of the Ravi, and now lies just inside Pakistan; it returned to the world's attention when [the corridor to it opened in 2019](/events/kartarpur-corridor-opens).
