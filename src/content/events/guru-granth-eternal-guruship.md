## The last camp at Nanded

Guru Gobind Singh travelled south with the emperor Bahadur Shah's camp through 1707–08, pressing a case for redress against the Punjab's governors, and halted at [Nanded](/places/nanded) on the Godavari.{{2,6}} There he was stabbed by an assassin — set on, the chronicles hold, by Wazir Khan of Sirhind, who had most to lose from the Guru's standing at court. The wound was stitched and appeared to heal, then reopened.

## The command

The best evidence for what followed is not a devotional text but a bookkeeping one. The Bhatt Vahis were working scrolls in which bards recorded the families they served — dates, births, deaths, visits — as professional record rather than praise. The *Bhatt Vahi Talauda Parganah Jind*, in an entry by Narbud Singh who was present, records the Guru sending Bhai Daya Singh to fetch the Granth on 7 October 1708, and the installation taking place on the 20th.{{1}}

The Guru placed the ceremonial offering of a coconut and five coins before the Adi Granth, bowed to it, and declared the line of personal Gurus closed. Authority passed to the scripture — thereafter the [Guru Granth Sahib](/faith/guru-granth-sahib) — and to the collective body of the initiated, the Guru Panth.{{1,2,7}} Takht Sri Hazur Sahib marks the place.

The command is carried in the couplet every Sikh congregation recites at the close of prayers: *sabh sikhan ko hukam hai, guru maneyo granth* — to all Sikhs the order is given, acknowledge the Granth as Guru.{{3}}

**What is disputed.** The couplet itself is preserved in Bhai Prahlad Singh's rahitnama rather than in the eyewitness record, and the rahitnama literature is later and internally inconsistent.{{3,5}} The dating varies too: some accounts place the Guru's death on 7 October, where the Bhatt Vahi entry has that as the date he sent for the Granth. The event is attested; the wording and the exact day come to us through transmission.

## Not a rupture but a conclusion

It is tempting to read 1708 as an abrupt substitution — a book handed the office a man had held. Gurinder Singh Mann argues it is better read as the end of a long argument.{{4}}

Guru Nanak held that his own compositions were revelation and that Sikhs should follow them; the Puratan Janam-sakhi already pictures him handing his *pothi* to Angad at the moment of nomination, the book as the token of succession.{{4}} Every contested succession afterward turned partly on manuscripts: Baba Mohan kept the Goindval Pothis and pressed a claim on the strength of them; Prithi Chand advanced his own by holding the volume said to be Guru Nanak's.{{4}} Possession of the text had been an argument about authority for a century and a half. In 1708 the argument was settled in the text's favour.

## A succession no one could seize

The decision answered the structural problem that had shadowed every succession since 1539 — a rival claimant splitting the community — by removing the office rivals were competing for. There could be no eleventh Guru to contest, corrupt, imprison or execute. What remained was a canon [fixed generations earlier](/events/installation-of-adi-granth) and a community [constituted at Vaisakhi 1699](/events/creation-of-the-khalsa), holding authority jointly.

For a people who had watched two Gurus killed in state custody, that was not only a theological settlement but a practical one. Weeks before his death the Guru had commissioned [Banda Singh Bahadur](/people/banda-singh-bahadur) to carry the struggle north, and [the next era](/history/sovereignty-banda-singh) begins there — fought by a community that no longer had a single head for anyone to remove.
