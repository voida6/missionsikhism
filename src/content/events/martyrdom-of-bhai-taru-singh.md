## The charge

Taru Singh farmed at Poola, near Kasur, and did what a great many Punjabi villagers did in the 1740s: he gave part of what he grew to the Sikh bands living out in the hills and the waste, where the [proscription](/events/martyrdom-of-bhai-mani-singh) had driven them.

He was informed on and taken to [Lahore](/places/lahore). The charge was not that he had fought — as far as the accounts go, he had not. It was that he had fed men who did.{{1,2}}

## The offer

He was imprisoned and tortured, then brought before the governor and offered the standard terms: convert, and live. In his case the conversion was to be demonstrated in a specific way — he was to cut off his *kes*, his uncut hair, and hand it over.

He refused. His scalp was then cut away from his skull with a knife, in public, so that the hair could never grow back. The date usually given is 9 June 1745. He lived for some weeks with the wound and died on 1 July. He was about twenty-five.{{1,2}}

## Why the demand took that form

The detail that the state wanted the hair specifically, rather than a formula of words, is the reason this execution is remembered as sharply as it is.

By the 1740s the outward form of the [Khalsa](/glossary/khalsa) had become the thing the administration could see, count and act on. Uncut hair identified a man on sight, and a man's hair in the governor's hand was proof of a conversion in a way a statement was not. The demand and the refusal were both about the same thing: whether the visible form was incidental to the faith or constitutive of it.

Purnima Dhavan's study of the century argues that Khalsa identity was still being worked out across exactly these decades rather than having been settled in 1699. Episodes like this one are part of how it was settled — under a knife, in public, with the state insisting the outward form mattered and Sikhs agreeing with it.

## The sources, and one detail

The account comes from the Sikh chronicle tradition, and it carries an element that plainly belongs to that tradition rather than to the record: that Taru Singh, dying, said Zakariya Khan would go before him and be killed by his shoes, and that the governor did die shortly after in circumstances the chronicles connect to it.{{3}}

That the execution happened, that its form was the scalping, and that the demand was for his hair are attested well beyond the devotional accounts and are not seriously questioned.
