## How it came about

The Punjab of the early 1980s combined a long-running dispute over water, territory and state autonomy — set out in the Anandpur Sahib Resolution of 1973 — with a rapidly worsening security situation and a central government that had repeatedly used and then abandoned Sikh political factions for electoral advantage.{{2,3}}

Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale, a preacher who had been encouraged early on by Congress figures as a counterweight to the Akali Dal, took up residence in the Golden Temple complex with armed followers, fortifying the Akal Takht. Assessments of him remain irreconcilable — a militant separatist, or a defender of Sikh rights driven into the complex — and this site does not pretend that is a settled question.{{2,3}}

## 1–8 June 1984

The Army entered [Amritsar](/places/amritsar) and assaulted the complex. The timing was 1–8 June, with the main action on the night of 5–6 June.

That timing matters. It was the martyrdom anniversary of [Guru Arjan](/events/martyrdom-of-guru-arjan) — the fifth Guru, who built the Harmandir Sahib — one of the days on which pilgrims come to the complex in the largest numbers. Whether that was deliberate, negligent or unavoidable is argued about; that it placed a large number of ordinary pilgrims inside a battlefield is not.

Tanks were brought into the precinct and fired on the [Akal Takht](/events/founding-of-akal-takht), which was very nearly destroyed. The Harmandir Sahib itself, which troops had orders to spare, took bullet damage. The Sikh Reference Library — manuscripts, hukamnamas, historical records, much of it irreplaceable — was burned after the fighting ended.{{2,4}}

## The figures

The government's White Paper gave 493 civilians and militants killed, 83 soldiers dead, and 1,592 people detained.{{1}}

Independent and community estimates run vastly higher — commonly 3,000 to 7,000, with some claims far above that.{{4}} The gap is not a matter of rounding. It reflects disagreement about how many pilgrims were inside, what happened to people detained afterwards, and how the dead were counted at all.

**This site does not adjudicate the number.** There has never been an independent inquiry with access to the evidence. What can be said with confidence is that the official figure is the lowest of the available estimates, that it was produced by the party responsible for the operation, and that no count has been accepted across the divide.

## What followed

On 31 October 1984 Indira Gandhi was assassinated by two of her Sikh bodyguards. In the days after, organised mobs killed Sikhs in Delhi and other Indian cities — some three thousand in Delhi alone by most counts — with widely documented police inaction and, in many accounts, participation by local political organisers. Convictions have been very few, and the pursuit of accountability continues four decades on.

Punjab then entered more than a decade of insurgency and counter-insurgency, with heavy loss of life on all sides and extensive documented abuses by security forces.{{5}}

The Akal Takht was rebuilt by the government soon after the operation. The community demolished that reconstruction and rebuilt it by *kar seva* — voluntary labour — because a seat of Sikh authority raised by the state that had shelled it was not acceptable as a seat of Sikh authority.

For a great many Sikhs, in India and across [the diaspora](/culture/global-community), 1984 is not history. It is the defining event of living memory, and the reason a substantial part of the community's relationship with the Indian state has never recovered.
