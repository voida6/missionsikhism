## The problem on both sides

By 1806 [Ranjit Singh](/people/ranjit-singh) had crossed the Sutlej more than once and was drawing the Sikh chiefs of the Cis-Sutlej states — Patiala, Nabha, Jind and the rest — towards [Lahore](/places/lahore). Unifying every Sikh territory under one authority was the obvious next move, and he was in a position to make it.{{1}}

The East India Company had a different problem. Napoleon and Persia were, on the intelligence then available in Calcutta, a plausible threat to India through the north-west, and the Company wanted the Punjab as a buffer rather than a rival. The Cis-Sutlej chiefs, meanwhile, preferred a distant protector to a near one and asked for British protection against Ranjit Singh.{{1,2}}

Charles Metcalfe was sent to Lahore to negotiate, arriving with a small escort and, behind him, a British force moved up to Ludhiana.

## The line

The treaty was concluded at [Amritsar](/places/amritsar) on 25 April 1809. Its substance is short: Ranjit Singh would not extend his authority south or east of the Sutlej; the Cis-Sutlej states passed under British protection; each side undertook to keep out of the other's territory.{{2,3}}

## What it bought and what it cost

It bought thirty-six years. The Sikh Empire and the Company did not fight until [1845](/events/first-anglo-sikh-war), and in the interval Ranjit Singh turned the whole force of the state in the only direction still open to it — north and west, to [Multan](/events/conquest-of-multan), Kashmir, Peshawar and [the Khyber](/events/battle-of-jamrud). Much of what the empire became, it became because this treaty closed the other door.{{1,2}}

The cost was a permanent division of the Sikh world at a river. The Cis-Sutlej states entered the British system in 1809 and stayed in it; the Punjab proper stayed sovereign for forty more years and was then [annexed](/events/british-annexation-of-punjab). The two halves acquired different administrations, different land settlements and different relationships to British power — a divergence still legible in the region.

## How it is read

There is a long argument about whether Ranjit Singh conceded too readily. The case for is that he was twenty-eight, had held Lahore for a decade, and gave up the unification of the Sikh territories without a fight. The case against is that the Company could concentrate more force than he could in 1809, that he knew it, and that he took the arrangement that let him build a state instead of losing one early.{{1,2}}

Grewal's judgement — that the treaty settled the empire's direction more than any battle did — is the one most often followed.{{2}}

The earliest full English narrative of the negotiation is Cunningham's, published in 1849 by an officer who went on to serve on the very frontier the treaty created — and who was [removed from his post](/events/first-anglo-sikh-war) for what he wrote about how that frontier was eventually crossed.{{5}}
