## The column at Kup

Ahmad Shah Durrani's sixth invasion of India, in early 1762, had a stated object: the destruction of Sikh power in the Punjab.{{1,2}}

Near Kup, outside Malerkotla, his fast-marching cavalry caught the Sikh column on the move. What it caught was not an army drawn up for battle. It was the *vahir* — the travelling body of a whole people, families and elderly and children and herds, moving with the fighting men rather than behind them, because there was no safe rear to leave them in. The fighting men formed a moving cordon and fought a running action across open country through the day of 5 February, dying in place while the column crawled toward the Malwa.{{2,3}}

The killing ran from Kup-Rohira through to Gehal, along the invader's line of march.{{4}}

## The toll, and the argument about it

Figures given for the dead range from about 5,000 to 50,000, with 25,000–30,000 the most commonly cited band — on the higher estimates, something between a third and a half of the entire Sikh population of the time.{{4,5}}

**What is disputed.** Two things, and both matter.

The first is the number. There was no census, no muster roll and no reliable count on either side, and the range above is wide enough that it is really a statement of uncertainty rather than a figure.

The second is harder. The account given above — that a large share of the dead were non-combatants travelling with the column — is the standard Sikh understanding and is what gives the day its particular horror. Some scholars note that the eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century sources do not explicitly record women and children on the field, and argue the engagement is better read as a military defeat of the Dal Khalsa.{{4}} The sources are not sufficient to settle it. This site gives the traditional account because it is the one the community holds and it is not contradicted by the record, while noting plainly that it is not independently established.

Later the same year Durrani returned to Amritsar, blew up the Harmandir Sahib and defiled the pool with the carcasses of slaughtered cattle.{{2}}

## What happened next

The recovery is the part that resists belief. Within months the misls were raiding to the gates of Sirhind and Lahore. By that autumn the community had reassembled at Amritsar in Durrani's despite. In 1764 Sirhind itself fell to the Khalsa, and in 1765 [the misls took Lahore](/events/sikh-capture-of-lahore) and struck coin in the Gurus' name.{{1,2}}

Two years separate the worst day in Sikh history from the capture of the city whose governor had ordered the [Guru's sons killed](/events/battle-of-chappar-chiri). Three separate it from the Punjab's capital.

This is why the Ghallughara sits in Sikh memory alongside [the martyrdoms of the Gurus](/events/martyrdom-of-guru-arjan) rather than as a defeat: catastrophe absorbed rather than submitted to. It is also why the community keeps the memory of 1762 as *vadda* — great — against the *chhota*, the lesser massacre of 1746. The naming assumes there will be a scale.
