## The rabab

Mardana was born at Talwandi — [Nankana Sahib](/places/nankana-sahib) — about ten years before [Guru Nanak](/people/guru-nanak), into a family of Muslim *mirasis*, hereditary musicians and genealogists. The two knew each other as boys in the same village.

When Nanak began the journeys the tradition calls the *udasis*, Mardana went with him and stayed for roughly four decades, across an itinerary that the janam-sakhis extend from Assam to Mecca. His instrument was the rabab, a plucked lute; Nanak composed, named the *raga* each composition belonged to, and Mardana played. The formula the accounts repeat — *Mardana, strike the rabab, the Word is descending* — is the tradition's own image of how the hymns came into the world: as music, before they were text.{{1,2,3}}

## What this made

That partnership set the pattern for [kirtan](/glossary/kirtan), the sung scripture that is still the central act of worship in a [gurdwara](/glossary/gurdwara). The Guru Granth Sahib is organised by musical measure rather than by author or theme — a structural fact that goes back to compositions made to be sung, with a musician present.

Three of Mardana's own verses were later included in the scripture, under Guru Angad's compilation and carried into the [Adi Granth of 1604](/events/installation-of-adi-granth). He died around 1534, at Kartarpur or on the road near Kurram, and Nanak is recorded as performing his last rites.{{1,2,3}}

## The point usually made about him

Nanak's closest lifelong companion was a Muslim, and remained one; there is no account of his conversion, and his verses sit in the Sikh scripture as they are. For a faith whose founding utterance was *there is no Hindu, there is no Musalman*, the forty-year working partnership at the centre of it is not incidental detail.{{1,2,3}}
