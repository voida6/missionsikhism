## The first to see it

Bibi Nanaki was born in 1464 at Chahal, five years before her brother [Nanak](/people/guru-nanak). The *janam-sakhis* — the traditional life-accounts — are consistent on one point across their variant tellings: while the household worried about a son who would not settle to a trade, would not keep accounts straight, and gave away what he was given, his sister took a different view of the same behaviour. She is remembered as the first person to accept him as more than an unusual boy, which makes her, in the tradition's own reckoning, the first Sikh.{{1,2,3}}

## Sultanpur Lodhi

Married to Jai Ram, an official at [Sultanpur Lodhi](/places/sultanpur-lodhi), she brought Nanak into that household and Jai Ram found him a post in the granary of the local Lodhi governor. It was at Sultanpur that Nanak worked, married, and — on the tradition's account — [disappeared into the Bein rivulet for three days and came out with the words that open the Sikh scripture](/events/enlightenment-at-sultanpur): *there is no Hindu, there is no Musalman.* She is also said to have given him his first rabab, the instrument on which [Bhai Mardana](/people/bhai-mardana) set his compositions.{{1,2,3}}

## What the evidence supports

The janam-sakhis were compiled well after the events they describe, and W. H. McLeod's work established how much of their detail is devotional narrative rather than recoverable biography. What survives that scrutiny is modest and still substantial: a sister, older, who backed Nanak materially and socially at the point when nobody else in the family did, and in whose house the decisive turn of his life took place.

That is why the tradition gives her the position it does. The faith did not begin with a crowd. It began with one person taking him seriously.{{1,2,3}}
