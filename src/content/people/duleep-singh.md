## A child on a collapsing throne

Duleep Singh was born at [Lahore](/places/lahore) in 1838, the youngest son of [Ranjit Singh](/people/ranjit-singh), months before his father died. The decade that followed consumed the Sikh Empire's leadership through a succession of murders and coups; by 1843 the survivors put the five-year-old on the throne, with his mother Maharani Jind Kaur as regent.

He was ten when the [Second Anglo-Sikh War ended in annexation](/events/british-annexation-of-punjab). The terms he was made to sign in 1849 surrendered the sovereignty of the Punjab, his claim to it, and the Koh-i-Noor diamond, which passed to Queen Victoria.{{1,2,3}}

## Removal

The administration separated him from his mother, who was imprisoned and then exiled; he did not see her for over a decade. Placed in the household of a Scottish surgeon, he was baptised a Christian at thirteen and sent to England at fifteen, where Victoria took a personal interest in him and he was settled as a country landowner at Elveden in Suffolk.{{1,2,3}}

## The return that did not happen

In middle age Duleep Singh set about undoing it. He pressed the India Office over the private estates he said had been wrongly taken, was refused, and in 1886 attempted to sail for India — he was detained at Aden and turned back. He was formally readmitted to the Sikh faith there, by the reckoning of his own account of it, having been baptised a Christian without any choice in the matter as a boy. He then spent years in Paris and Moscow seeking Russian backing for a rising in the Punjab that never came near being possible.

He died in Paris in 1893, largely broke, and was buried at Elveden under a Christian rite, which was not what he had asked for.{{1,2,3}}

## Why he is in the chronicle

Duleep Singh matters less for what he did than for what was done to him, and how legible it is. A sovereign's child was deposed, converted, expatriated and pensioned, and the paperwork survives for all of it. His life is the annexation of the Punjab carried out on one person, and it is the reason the [Koh-i-Noor](/history/sikh-empire) remains an open argument rather than a closed piece of history.{{1,2,3}}
