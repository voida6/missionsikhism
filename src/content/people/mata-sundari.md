## The forty years nobody plans for

[Guru Gobind Singh](/people/guru-gobind-singh) died at [Nanded](/places/nanded) in 1708, having [vested the Guruship permanently in the Guru Granth Sahib](/events/guru-granth-eternal-guruship). The doctrine was settled. The practical question was not: who, day to day, would hold a community that had just lost its living leader, was under Mughal proscription, and would shortly be scattered by the campaigns that followed [Banda Singh Bahadur](/people/banda-singh-bahadur)?

For most of the next four decades the answer was Mata Sundari, from Delhi.{{1,2,3}}

## What she actually did

Her authority was administrative and moral rather than doctrinal, and she was careful about the distinction. She issued *hukamnamas* — letters of instruction carrying her seal — to congregations across the Punjab and beyond. She confirmed appointments, settled disputes between groups claiming to speak for the Panth, and directed the collection and use of offerings. She had Bhai Mani Singh sent to [Amritsar](/places/amritsar) to take charge of the Harmandir Sahib and its establishment, at a point when the shrine's management was contested.

Through the same years she rejected the succession claims that inevitably appeared — including those made on behalf of the Bandai Khalsa — on the ground that the Guruship was closed. That she held this line, from the imperial capital, for forty years is the reason the [Khalsa](/glossary/khalsa) came out of the eighteenth century with one scripture and no rival lineage of living Gurus.{{1,2,3}}

## The record

Her *hukamnamas* survive, which is why this is documented history rather than tradition: they are dated, sealed, and addressed to named congregations, and they are among the better evidence for how the Panth was actually governed between 1708 and the rise of the [misls](/history/misl-period). She died at Delhi in 1747, the year Ahmad Shah Abdali began the invasions that would define the next Sikh generation.{{1,2,3}}
