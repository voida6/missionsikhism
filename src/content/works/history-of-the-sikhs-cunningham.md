Cunningham was a British political officer on the Sutlej and was at the battles
he writes about. His book is the first substantial account of the Sikhs in
English that treats them as a subject in their own right rather than as an
obstacle, and it remains a source for the Anglo-Sikh wars precisely because of
where its author was standing.

**What it cost him.** In the final chapters Cunningham criticised Lord
Hardinge's Punjab policy and stated plainly that two of the Sikh commanders in
the First Anglo-Sikh War, Lal Singh and Tej Singh, had been bought. For this he
was removed from his political appointment and returned to regimental duty,
losing his income and any prospect of advancement. He died at Ambala in 1851.

Read him as a participant rather than a neutral, which is how he asks to be
read. The reason the betrayal at Ferozeshah and Sobraon is treated as
established rather than as Sikh grievance is that a British officer on the
winning side put it in print in 1849 and was punished for it.
