Written in Persian verse in 1705 from the Malwa — Kangar in present-day Bathinda
district, or nearby Dina — and carried to the emperor by Bhai Daya Singh and
Bhai Dharam Singh. It runs to 111 verses. Thirty-four of them are given to the
praise of God; verses 19 to 41 narrate the
[battle at Chamkaur](/events/battle-of-chamkaur); fifteen concern one thing
specifically, which is that the safe passage out of
[Anandpur](/events/evacuation-of-anandpur) had been sworn on the Qur'an and then
broken.

Its importance as a source is hard to overstate and easy to miss. Sikh history
before the nineteenth century is mostly reconstructed from chronicles written
later, from hostile Mughal records, or from tradition. This is neither: it is the
central figure of the events, writing at the time, in his own name, to the man
he is accusing.

It is also not a plea. The Guru writes as one sovereign addressing another,
tells Aurangzeb that a ruler who breaks an oath sworn on his own scripture has
forfeited the claim to rule by it, and titles the letter the Epistle of Victory
while in flight with his family dead.

The text is preserved within the Dasam Granth, and is among the very few
Persian-language compositions to hold that place in the Sikh canon.
